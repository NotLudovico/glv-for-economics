\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis asked whether a dynamical model of interacting firms can produce the two
firm-growth anomalies by itself, without the heavy-tailed internal composition that the
granular models assume and that \citet{moran2024} show the data reject. This thesis shows
that it can. A Generalized Lotka--Volterra network, with no external heterogeneity, no injected
noise, and no built-in correlations, recovers both the fat-tailed growth-rate distribution and a
size--variance relation that falls with the empirical sign. Both come out as stationary
properties of an economy that grows while its composition keeps fluctuating.

The model is one change to the standard Generalized Lotka--Volterra system.
Self-limitation and interactions act on a firm's size relative to the average firm rather
than on its absolute size. This makes the dynamics scale-invariant. Total output then grows
exponentially, without the finite-time blow-up of the absolute model, and in the strongly
disordered regime the composition of the economy never settles but keeps churning
chaotically. In that state the two facts hold together, and they prove robust. They
survive as the graph, the system size, and the sampling interval are varied, and
switching off the disorder removes them entirely. Their origin is therefore in the
interactions between firms, and no property of a single firm accounts for them. The change was forced by what the
absolute model cannot do. Tuned to its cooperative critical point, that model reproduces
the heavy tails, but its size--variance relation is only a relaxation transient, the trace
of an arbitrary initial condition settling onto the critical attractor. Recovering a genuine law needs a state that keeps fluctuating instead of relaxing.
That analysis, together with the coordinate change and time rescaling that make the
critical point reachable at all, is collected in the appendices.

The thesis contributes a model. It does not claim to read a mechanism off the data. The
granular and sub-unit pictures posit the source of the fluctuations, a fixed heavy-tailed
composition of parts within each firm; the mean-field multiplicative models that also
give a growing economy with heavy tails put the noise in by hand and carry no network
\citep{bouchaudmezard2000}. Here the same phenomenology, the heavy tails, the slow
size--variance decay, the growing aggregate, and the constant turnover of firms, follows
from one deterministic ingredient, the disordered couplings between companies. The
size-dependent correlations that \citet{moran2024} name as the missing element are then
generated by the model itself, which turns them from a hypothesis one has to accept into
something one can measure. The account moves from within the firm, where a company's
volatility is fixed by how its own activities are composed, to between firms, where it
comes from the company's place in a fluctuating web of competitors. That is the more
natural home for the shared customers and suppliers to which the correlations were
attributed in the first place.

Three qualifications bound the claim. The dynamics themselves are not new. The fluctuating
regime is the chaotic phase of the disordered Lotka--Volterra model, equivalently the disordered
replicator equation \citep{diederich1989,bunin2017,galla2018,roy2020}, and its
finite-size fragility and approach to a robust mean-field fluctuating phase are already
studied. What is specific here is the construction that turns that state into a growing
economy, the relative interactions, and its use as a model of firm growth. The
quantitative match, second, is partial, and partial in an informative way. The model
reproduces the order-dependent multiscaling of the higher moments, the signature of
size-growing correlations that the granular models cannot produce; the size--variance
exponent it matches only in sign. That exponent is a clean, size-independent power law,
about four times too steep, $\beta \approx 0.8$ against the empirical $0.15$--$0.20$. The
per-firm scaling that quiets the disorder field into the correctly multiscaling
volatility distribution is also what over-stabilizes the most connected firms and steepens
the exponent, so the steep magnitude and the correct multiscaling come out of the same
scaling and cannot be tuned apart, which points past the single-level model. Third, the
growth is near-critical. The operating point that best reproduces the facts sits close to
the boundary between a growing and a shrinking economy, so the economy grows in most graph
realizations but not in all. None of this undoes the qualitative result, but each
qualification marks the distance between it and a quantitative theory.

The next steps follow from these limits. Fixing the exponent and the near-critical
character of the growth would take many more realizations at the largest system sizes,
where the present estimates are noisiest. The correlation structure matters more than
either. The match shown here is in the marginal facts and their moments, the shape of the growth
distribution and the scaling of its volatility; the size-dependent correlations that
\citet{moran2024} call for are something the model generates directly and could measure
directly, firm by firm, rather than only through their consequences. That measurement,
together with closing the gap in the exponent's magnitude, points to the ingredient the
single-level model still lacks, a firm built from many correlated parts. Further out,
replacing the schematic disordered graph with couplings closer to the real economy, its
input--output linkages, supply networks, and shared markets, would be the step from a
model that reproduces the stylized facts to one that speaks to the macroeconomic dynamics
behind them.
