\begin{abstract}
Firm growth shows two robust anomalies. First, the volatility of a firm's growth rate
falls with size much more slowly than independence would predict: $\sigma(S)\sim
S^{-\beta}$ with $\beta\approx0.15$--$0.20$ rather than $1/2$. Second, the growth-rate
distribution is sharply peaked and fat-tailed rather than Gaussian. The standard
explanations place the source of these effects inside each firm, in a heavy-tailed
composition of internal sub-units. \citet{moran2024} test this granular picture against
firm data and find that it fails, and argue that the missing ingredient is correlations
that grow with a firm's size. We ask instead whether a dynamical model of interacting
firms can generate the same facts on its own, with no heterogeneity, noise, or
correlations imposed from outside, so that any size-dependent structure is produced by
the dynamics rather than assumed.

We model the economy as a Generalized Lotka--Volterra system of firms on a sparse random
graph. Held at its cooperative critical point the standard model reproduces the
fat-tailed growth-rate distribution on heterogeneous graphs, but its size--variance
relation is only a relaxation transient, not a stationary law, and so offers no exponent
to compare with the data; recovering one calls for a state that keeps fluctuating rather
than relaxing. We obtain it with a single change to the model, letting the
self-limitation and the interactions act on the average firm rather than on absolute
abundances. This makes the dynamics scale-invariant: the economy grows exponentially
without ever blowing up, while in the strongly disordered regime its composition never
settles but keeps churning. In this persistently fluctuating state the two facts appear
together as stationary properties, a symmetric fat-tailed growth distribution and a
size--variance relation that falls with the empirical sign, and they are robust across
graph realizations, system size, and the sampling interval. Switching off the disorder
removes both, which locates their origin in the interactions themselves rather than in
any single-firm mechanism.

Deterministic couplings between firms can therefore generate the firm-growth anomalies
endogenously, together with a steadily growing economy. The size--variance exponent comes
out with the right sign, and its higher moments reproduce the anomalous multiscaling that
the granular models cannot, the signature of size-growing correlations; its magnitude,
however, is matched only up to the normalization of the couplings, with no single choice
reproducing both the magnitude and the multiscaling, a trade-off that itself points to
the missing ingredient.
\end{abstract}
