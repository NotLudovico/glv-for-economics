\chapter{Introduction}

A well-known stylized fact about firms is how the volatility of their growth rate
depends on their size. If you measure the standard deviation of a firm's growth
rate, it goes down with size as $\sigma(S) \propto S^{-\beta}$, and the exponent
comes out around $\beta \approx 0.15$--$0.20$ in the data \citep{stanley1996,
amaral1997}. The naive expectation is quite different. If a firm were just a bag of
$N$ independent sub-units, each fluctuating on its own, the central limit theorem
would give $\beta = 0.5$. The fact that the measured exponent is so much smaller
tells us the sub-units are not independent: diversification inside a firm works far
worse than simple averaging would suggest. Explaining this slow decay, and at the
same time the fat-tailed, non-Gaussian shape of the growth-rate distribution, is the
problem this thesis starts from.

The idea that ties this together is \emph{granularity}. Normally we expect the
idiosyncratic shocks hitting $N$ comparable units to average out as $1/\sqrt{N}$, so
that anything left at the macroscopic scale has to come from genuinely aggregate
shocks. \citet{gabaix2011} pointed out that this is no longer true when the units
have a fat-tailed (Zipf-like) size distribution. In that case a few big units make
up a finite fraction of the total, their own shocks do not get washed out by the
others, and firm-level randomness shows up directly in the aggregate. He estimated
that just the largest hundred US firms account for about a third of the variance of
output growth. So what matters is the \emph{concentration} of the size distribution,
not how many units there are. The same argument can be pushed down one level, to a
single firm seen as the sum of its own parts.

This is exactly what the ``sub-business'' models do. A firm is treated as a
collection of internal units---divisions, products, plants---and its growth rate is
the size-weighted sum of theirs. \citet{sutton2002} called the slow size--volatility
decay a ``scaling puzzle'' and tried to explain it from the internal structure of the
firm, and \citet{wyartbouchaud2003} showed that if you give the sub-units a
heavy-tailed size distribution you get both anomalies at once: the variance of firm
growth decays too slowly with size, and the growth-rate distribution stays
non-Gaussian and fat-tailed instead of becoming Gaussian as the number of sub-units
grows. The firm-level puzzle therefore has the same granular origin as the aggregate
one---it is the spread in the sizes of the constituents, not their number, that
decides how well diversification works.

\citet{moran2024} recently put this picture together more carefully. They write the
growth variance of a firm as $\sigma^2 = \sigma_0^2\, H$, where $H$ is the Herfindahl
index measuring how concentrated the firm's sales are across its sub-units. When the
sub-unit sizes have a heavy tail with exponent $1 < \mu < 2$, $H$ decays very slowly
with the number of sub-units $K$---much slower than the $H \sim 1/K$ you would get
with a thin tail---and this slow decay is what produces $\beta < 1/2$ and the
L\'evy-stable shape of the growth-rate distribution. In other words the anomalous
exponent comes from \emph{structural} heterogeneity: a few dominant sub-units carry
the firm, so adding more of them does not really average out the risk. They also show
that the link between concentration and volatility depends on the correlation between
a sub-unit's size and its volatility---the anomalous scaling only shows up when this
correlation is not perfect---and in French firm data, industries where size and
volatility are more correlated have a steeper size--volatility decay. This framework
reproduces both the scaling and the shape of the distribution, and it is the current
reference explanation for these facts.

All of this works, but it puts the source of the correlations \emph{inside} each
firm, in a fixed, externally chosen list of sub-units. In this thesis we drop the
sub-unit picture entirely. Instead of breaking a firm into internal pieces and
blaming its volatility on how concentrated they are, we model the economy directly as
a network of interacting \emph{firms}: every node of the graph is a company, and the
edges are the competitive and cooperative couplings between them. The question we ask
is whether the same empirical facts can come out of this inter-firm picture---which
seems to us more economically natural---where the correlations are not put in by hand
but are produced \emph{dynamically} by the interactions between companies. The
standard model for this kind of interaction is the Generalized Lotka--Volterra (GLV)
system, borrowed from theoretical ecology: each node grows logistically and is
coupled to its neighbors through a signed interaction matrix encoding competition and
cooperation. So instead of assuming a heavy-tailed firm composition, we let a network
of inter-firm couplings generate the heterogeneity on its own. The catch is that a
deterministic GLV system does not usually keep fluctuating---away from equilibrium its
restoring forces just damp everything out. The interesting case is the critical point
$\mu = \mu_c$, where the interior fixed point loses stability, the restoring forces
go to zero, and you get long-lived, scale-spanning transient fluctuations. That is the
regime we want to study: \emph{can a static GLV network tuned to its critical point
produce the inter-firm correlations needed to recover the empirical scaling, as an
endogenous alternative to the granular mechanism?}

Sitting right at $\mu_c$ is also exactly where a naive simulation falls apart. As
$\mu \to \mu_c$ the total abundance grows without bound, and for $\mu > \mu_c$ it
actually diverges in finite time, which forces any explicit ODE solver into tiny step
sizes or floating-point overflow. The first part of this thesis is a numerical method
that gets around this instead of fighting it. We split the dynamics into a bounded
``shape'' part living on the probability simplex and an unbounded ``scale'' part
carrying the total abundance, and rescale time so that the finite-time blow-up in
physical time becomes a smooth growth in a new time variable. The shape dynamics can
then be integrated stably straight through the critical point, and the physical
trajectories reconstructed for as long as the scale stays representable. With this
tool in hand, the second part is a systematic check of whether critical GLV dynamics,
on a few different network topologies, actually reproduce the empirical
size--volatility scaling and the heavy tails of the growth-rate distribution. So the
thesis gives both a numerical framework that makes the critical regime reachable in
the first place and, using it, a clean answer to what static topological criticality
can and cannot say about firm-growth fluctuations.

The rest of the thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:rescaling} works
through the coordinate change in detail, mapping the diverging GLV equations onto the
probability simplex, and discusses the limits of the mean-field approximations.
Chapter~\ref{chap:results} presents the numerical method and the results, showing how
the divergence is bypassed and analyzing the scaling behavior across topologies.
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} concludes and sketches the theoretical extensions that
would be needed to get realistic macroeconomic dynamics.
