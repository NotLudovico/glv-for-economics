\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

A well-known stylized fact about firms concerns how the volatility of their growth
depends on their size. If one measures, year over year, how much a firm's growth rate
fluctuates, this volatility falls with firm size as $\sigma(S) \propto S^{-\beta}$, and
across datasets the exponent is small, $\beta \approx 0.15$--$0.20$
\citep{stanley1996, amaral1997}. The value is anomalous because of what it is not. If a
firm of size $S$ were a collection of many independent activities of comparable size,
the central limit theorem would average their idiosyncratic shocks down as one over the
square root of their number, giving $\beta = 1/2$. The measured exponent is less than
half of that, so the fluctuations of large firms die away far more slowly than
independence would allow: diversification, which ought to make large firms quiet, barely
works. Because firm growth is the very process by which the size distribution of firms
and the aggregate output of an economy evolve, this slow decay is not a curiosity of
microdata. It says that firm-level shocks do not wash out at the macroscopic scale, so
that the idiosyncratic fortunes of individual companies leave a fingerprint on the
fluctuations of the whole economy.

A second regularity accompanies the first. The distribution of growth rates is not
Gaussian but sharply peaked at zero, with broad, roughly exponential flanks that span
several decades, a tent shape on a logarithmic scale \citep{stanley1996, amaral1997}.
Unlike a Gaussian it does not smooth into a bell as activities are aggregated; the heavy
tails persist at every size, and a firm's growth remains liable to large jumps however
large the firm. The two facts, the slow size--variance decay and the fat-tailed,
non-Gaussian growth distribution, are robust across countries, industries and decades,
and any model that claims to explain firm growth must produce both at once rather than
either alone.

The standard explanation of both is granular, and it is most easily seen one level up,
at the scale of the whole economy. The idiosyncratic shocks hitting $N$ comparable units
are expected to average out as $1/\sqrt{N}$, so that anything surviving at the
macroscopic scale must come from genuinely aggregate shocks. \citet{gabaix2011} pointed
out that this expectation fails when the units have a fat-tailed, Zipf-like size
distribution. In that case a handful of the largest units account for a finite fraction
of the total, their own shocks are no longer cancelled by the rest, and firm-level
randomness appears directly in the aggregate. Gabaix estimated that the hundred largest
US firms alone account for about a third of the variance of output growth. What matters
is the concentration of the size distribution, not the sheer number of units, and the
same argument can be pushed down one level, from an economy made of firms to a firm made
of its own parts.

This is what the sub-business models do. A firm is treated as a collection of internal
units, divisions, products or plants, and its growth rate is the size-weighted sum of
theirs. \citet{sutton2002} called the slow size--volatility decay a scaling puzzle and
sought its origin in this internal structure. \citet{wyartbouchaud2003} then showed that
endowing the sub-units with a heavy-tailed size distribution delivers both anomalies
together: the variance of firm growth decays too slowly with size, and the growth-rate
distribution stays non-Gaussian and fat-tailed rather than Gaussianizing as the number
of sub-units grows. On this view the firm-level puzzle has the same granular origin as
the aggregate one. The spread in the sizes of a firm's constituents, not their number,
decides how well diversification works, and a firm whose sales are concentrated in a few
large activities behaves like a small one however large it is in total.

Recently \citet{moran2024} revisited this granular picture and, crucially, tested it
rather than only fitting it. They first unify the aggregate argument of \citet{gabaix2011}
and the firm-level one of \citet{wyartbouchaud2003} into a single framework, writing the
growth variance of a firm as $\sigma^2 = \sigma_0^2\, H$, where $H$ is the Herfindahl
index measuring how concentrated the firm's sales are across its sub-units. A
heavy-tailed sub-unit size distribution, with exponent $1 < \mu < 2$, makes $H$ decay
slowly with the number of sub-units and reproduces both marginal facts at once, a
size--variance exponent below one half and a fat-tailed growth distribution. Judged on
the two stylized facts alone, the granular picture works.

Its value as an explanation, however, rests on sharper predictions that reach past those
marginal facts to the mechanism beneath them. The same framework predicts that the
volatility, once rescaled by its size-dependent average, should have a distribution
across firms that is universal, the same shape at every size; and that the growth rates,
rescaled by each firm's own volatility, should converge to a Gaussian, since a firm built
from many sub-units is a sum of many contributions and the central limit theorem ought to
apply to it as to any other sum. These are the decisive tests, because they ask not
whether the granular model can be tuned to the two laws but whether the object it posits,
a firm that is a sum of independent heavy-tailed parts, actually describes the data.

It does not. \citet{moran2024} find that the rescaled volatility distribution is indeed
universal, as predicted, but that its tail is far too thin to match the granular
prediction: real firms' volatilities are far more alike than a heavy-tailed composition
of sub-units would make them. And the rescaled growth rates do not Gaussianize. They stay
fat-tailed at every size, the largest firms no closer to a Gaussian than the smallest,
the exact opposite of what a growing sum of independent parts must do. A third and
sharper diagnostic concerns the higher moments. The $q$-th moment of the size-conditioned
volatility decays as a power of size with an exponent $\zeta_q$, and a granular
composition of independent parts predicts a single exponent independent of $q$. The data
instead show $\zeta_q$ rising with the order $q$, an anomalous multiscaling that no sum of
independent contributions can produce. On every test that looks past the two marginal
laws to the structure beneath them, the granular picture is rejected.

The conclusion \citet{moran2024} draw is that granularity alone cannot account for firm
growth, and that the missing ingredient is correlations between a firm's parts that grow
with the firm's size, whether from customers and suppliers shared across activities or
from shocks that strike the whole firm at once. The anomalies are, on this reading, the
fingerprint of size-growing correlations, exactly what a model of independent parts
lacks. The natural question is where such correlations come from, and it is here that the
present work parts company with the sub-unit tradition.

The correlations the granular picture needs are placed inside the individual firm, among
a fixed and externally chosen list of sub-units, even though the sources invoked for
them, shared customers and suppliers, are in truth links between firms. In this thesis we
drop the sub-unit picture altogether. Rather than break a firm into internal pieces and
attribute its volatility to their concentration, we model the economy directly as a
network of interacting firms: every node of a graph is a company, and every edge a
competitive or cooperative coupling between two of them. We then ask whether the same
empirical facts can emerge from this inter-firm picture, in which the correlations are not
imposed by hand but produced by the dynamics of the interactions themselves. The standard
model of such interactions is the Generalized Lotka--Volterra (GLV) system borrowed from
theoretical ecology, in which each node grows logistically and is coupled to its
neighbours through a signed interaction matrix encoding competition and cooperation.
Instead of assuming a heavy-tailed composition within each firm, we let a disordered
network of inter-firm couplings generate the heterogeneity on its own.

A deterministic GLV does not by itself keep fluctuating: away from equilibrium its
restoring forces damp every perturbation, and the firm-growth fluctuations we are after
require a state in which they do not. Tuning the standard GLV to its cooperative critical
point, where those restoring forces vanish, is a natural first attempt, and it does
reproduce the fat-tailed growth distribution on heterogeneous graphs. But it fails on the
other fact: at the critical point the size--variance relation is not a stationary law but
a relaxation transient, a curve traced as an arbitrary initial condition settles onto the
critical attractor, after which the growth rates vanish and no exponent is left to compare
with the data. That analysis, together with the coordinate change and time rescaling
needed to integrate the model through its critical blow-up, is collected in
Appendices~\ref{chap:rescaling} and~\ref{chap:results}; its lesson is that a stationary
size--variance law calls for a state that keeps fluctuating rather than relaxing. The
model of this thesis is built to provide one, through a single change to the GLV: the
self-limitation and the interactions act on a firm's size relative to the average firm
rather than on its absolute size. This makes the dynamics scale-invariant. The total
output of the economy then grows exponentially without ever diverging, and in the
strongly disordered regime the composition of the economy never settles but keeps churning
chaotically. In this persistently fluctuating state the two empirical facts appear
together as genuine stationary properties, a fat-tailed growth distribution and a
size--variance relation that falls with the empirical sign, and they survive across graph
realizations, system sizes and the interval over which growth is measured. Switching off
the disorder removes both at once, which locates their origin in the interactions and not
in any property of a single firm.

The contribution of the thesis is therefore not to confirm that size-dependent
correlations are the missing ingredient, which \citet{moran2024} already argue from the
data, but to give a first-principles dynamical model of interacting firms from which the
firm-growth anomalies, and with them a steadily growing economy of perpetually
turning-over firms, emerge on their own. The model reaches past the two marginal facts:
it reproduces the anomalous multiscaling of the higher moments, the very signature the
granular models cannot, generating the size-growing correlations rather than assuming
them. The size--variance exponent itself comes out with the right sign, though its
magnitude is matched only up to the normalization of the couplings, a gap we trace to a
genuine trade-off rather than to a free parameter left loose.

The rest of the thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:growing} sets out the
model. It introduces the relative GLV and its scale-invariant growth, locates the chaotic,
growing regime in which the firm-growth statistics are measured, and supports that picture
with a dynamical mean-field theory, derived and validated against simulation, that predicts
the phase structure in closed form. Chapter~\ref{chap:firmgrowth} measures the two
firm-growth facts in the fluctuating state, examines their robustness across system size
and the approach to the mean-field limit, and turns to the higher moments, where the
multiscaling that separates the data from the granular models is recovered. Chapter
~\ref{chap:conclusion} draws the results together and points to what would tie the model
more tightly to the data, in particular a direct measurement of the size-dependent
correlations it generates. Five appendices hold the supporting material: the absolute
model and the integration of its critical point (Appendices~\ref{chap:rescaling}
and~\ref{chap:results}), the derivation of the rescaled equations
(Appendix~\ref{app:derivation}), the dynamical mean-field theory (Appendix~\ref{app:dmft}),
and the numerical methods and measurement protocol (Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}).
