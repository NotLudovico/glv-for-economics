\chapter{Firm-growth statistics across network topologies}
\label{chap:results}

With the rescaled integrator of Chapter~\ref{chap:rescaling} we can hold the GLV
system exactly at its cooperative critical point and read off long, scale-spanning
transients that an explicit solver could never resolve. This chapter uses that tool
to ask the question posed in the introduction. Does a static GLV network tuned to
$\mu_c$ reproduce the two empirical firm-growth regularities (the slow
size--volatility decay $\sigma(S)\sim S^{-\beta}$ with $\beta\approx0.15$--$0.20$, and
the fat-tailed, non-Gaussian growth-rate distribution), and how do the answers depend
on the topology of the inter-firm network?

We use three degree distributions at fixed mean degree $C=5$, namely a regular graph
($k=C$ for all nodes, no degree variance), an exponential configuration model
($k\sim\mathrm{Exp}(C)$), and a power-law graph ($p(k)\propto k^{-\alpha}$ with
$\alpha=2.5$ and the lower cutoff set so the mean degree tracks $C$). Throughout we fix
$N=1000$ and disorder $\sigma=0.2$, place the system at the per-topology critical point
$\mu_c$ located with the shape-scalar estimator of Section~\ref{sec:estimator-c}, and
average each observable over $40$ independent graph realizations.

\section{Observables}
\label{sec:observables}

We treat each node as a firm and its abundance as the firm's size. To mirror how the
empirical exponents are measured we normalize sizes within each cross-section. Writing
$\tilde{S}_{iy}$ for the raw abundance of firm $i$ in year $y$, the normalized size is
\begin{equation}
    S_{iy} = N\,\frac{\tilde{S}_{iy}}{\sum_j \tilde{S}_{jy}},
    \label{eq:size-norm}
\end{equation}
so the cross-sectional mean size is $1$ in every year and the common-mode growth of
the total abundance, which at $\mu_c$ is precisely the diverging envelope $M(t)$, is
divided out, leaving only the relative reshuffling of firms. This is the
cross-sectional normalization of \citet{moran2024}. The (log) growth rate and its
time-averaged volatility are
\begin{equation}
    g_{iy} = \ln\frac{S_{i,y+1}}{S_{iy}}, \qquad
    \sigma_i = \sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac{1}{T_i}\sum_y \bigl|\,g_{iy}-\bar{g}_i\,\bigr|,
    \label{eq:vol-mad}
\end{equation}
where the mean-absolute-deviation estimator (rescaled by $\sqrt{\pi/2}$, the
Gaussian-consistent factor) is used in place of the standard deviation for robustness
against the fat tails of $g$. Physical-time trajectories $x(t)=M(t)\,y(t)$ are
reconstructed from a rescaled run and sampled on a uniform grid of $100$ ``years''
between $t=0$ and the (finite) blow-up time $t^\ast$. At $\mu_c$ with modest disorder
the relative sizes disperse only mildly and
never collapse (no firm goes extinct), which is what makes the size--variance fit
below well-conditioned over the whole population rather than a surviving subset.

\section{The size--variance relation}
\label{sec:sizevar-baseline}

We work at $\mu_c$, the operating point fixed in Section~\ref{sec:locating-muc}; it is
forced by the dynamics and not tuned to fit anything here. We pool the per-firm pairs
$(\bar{S}_i,\sigma_i)$ over all realizations and bin them by size. The result
(Figure~\ref{fig:res-sizevar-baseline}, exponential graph, $C=5$) is not a power law.
The volatility falls with size down to a minimum near the mean size $\bar{S}\approx1$
and then rises again in a V shape. A single power-law fit forced through
it returns $\beta\approx0.5$, but that number averages the two opposite slopes and
does not describe the data; the relation has no single exponent. The same V shape
appears across the topologies and connectivities we examined. The next section shows
where the V comes from. It is a relaxation transient, left behind as the system settles
from its initial condition, and no stationary size--variance law underlies it.

\section{The volatility as a relaxation transient}
\label{sec:relaxation-transient}

We have called $\sigma_i$ a volatility. What it actually measures becomes concrete
in the rescaled dynamics. The shape
equation~\eqref{eq:dydtau} is autonomous and deterministic. It carries no
stochastic forcing, and the scale $M$ is slaved to it through
\eqref{eq:dMdtau}--\eqref{eq:dtdtau}. On the simplex the flow relaxes to a fixed point
$y^\star$ (Chapter~\ref{chap:rescaling}); once it has, the normalized sizes
$S_{iy}$~\eqref{eq:size-norm} are constant and every log-growth $g_{iy}$ in
\eqref{eq:vol-mad} vanishes identically. A nonzero $\sigma_i$ can therefore arise
only while the system relaxes from its initial condition toward the critical
attractor. The size--variance relation is therefore a property of that transient, and
it disappears once the system reaches a stationary state.

Three controlled interventions, run on the same graphs, make this concrete.

\paragraph{The volatility appears early.} Partitioning the rescaled-time window into
quarters, the median $\sigma_i$ across the four quarters is $[\,0.007,\,0,\,0,\,0\,]$. Essentially all of the measured fluctuation is
produced in the first quarter, after which the firms stay at $y^\star$.

\paragraph{Starting at equilibrium gives no volatility.} Initialising every firm at its own fixed
point, $S_i(0)=S_i^\star$, yields $\sigma_i=0$ identically. The measured volatility is
just the log-distance a firm travels between its initial and equilibrium sizes,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_i \;\sim\; \bigl|\,\ln S_i^\star - \ln S_i(0)\,\bigr|,
    \label{eq:sigma-journey}
\end{equation}
weighted by the (mildly size-dependent) relaxation rate; when the start is decorrelated
from the destination, $\sigma_i$ tracks the journey $|\ln(S_i^\star/S_i(0))|$ more
closely than the destination $|\ln S_i^\star|$ alone.

\paragraph{The minimum sits where the firms start.} The cross-sectional
normalisation~\eqref{eq:size-norm} pins the mean of $S_i(0)$ to unity, so every firm
begins near $S\approx1$. By~\eqref{eq:sigma-journey} the firms that move least, and so
register the smallest $\sigma_i$, are those whose equilibrium also lies near $1$, while
firms equilibrating far above or below travel further and fluctuate more. The result is
a minimum of $\sigma(\bar S)$ at the mean size, with a diversification-like decay
below it and a self-limitation-like rise above; both branches are the same relaxation
seen from either side. That this minimum is fixed by the initial condition, and not by
any intrinsic scale, follows from displacing it. Lowering the typical starting size
$S_0$ (with log-normal initial conditions of increasing spread, which depress the
median of $S_i(0)$ while the cross-section holds its mean at $1$) slides the minimum to
follow, from $\bar S\approx1.07$ at $S_0\approx1.0$ down to $\bar S\approx0.46$ at
$S_0\approx0.22$ (Figure~\ref{fig:res-ic-relaxation}). An intrinsic, size-dependent
volatility cannot be relocated by changing where the firms start.

None of this can be the empirical size--variance law, for three reasons. First, the V
is the wrong shape. The data show $\sigma(S)$ falling monotonically across the whole
size range, whereas the V turns around and rises, so in the model the largest firms
are the most volatile, the opposite of what is observed; and no choice of topology,
connectivity, or initial condition removes the rising branch, since it is simply the
half of the population that equilibrates above where it started. Second, the V is
generic rather than dynamical. Because $\sigma_i$ measures how far a firm travels from
the common starting size (Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma-journey}), any relaxation from a shared
initial condition gives a minimum at the start and rising flanks on either side,
whatever the interactions are; the single slope a power-law fit reports is only where
the two branches happen to balance, and it slides with the initial condition. The
shape is therefore a property of the measurement rather than of the network, and contains no
size--variance exponent to read. Third, the V is transient. The shape relaxes to a
fixed point, where every growth rate is exactly zero, so a deterministic GLV resting
there has no stationary size--variance law to offer at all. The empirical $\sigma(S)$
is a property of an economy that keeps fluctuating; reproducing it requires a genuinely
nonstationary state, an explicit noise term (a Langevin GLV) or the chaotic $\sigma>1$
phase rather than the critical relaxation measured here. This sharpens the closing
remark of Section~\ref{sec:results-discussion}.

\section{The growth-rate distribution}
\label{sec:growthdist}

The second empirical fact is the shape of the growth-rate distribution, which in the
data is sharply peaked and fat-tailed rather than Gaussian. Here the model does
better, though only qualitatively and only on the heterogeneous graphs.
Figure~\ref{fig:res-growthdist} overlays the pooled growth rates for the three
topologies, plotted raw, without per-series rescaling, as in \citet{moran2024}. The
exponential and power-law graphs are sharply peaked at zero with broad, roughly
exponential (tent-shaped) flanks spanning several decades; this reproduces the
qualitative form reported by \citet{stanley1996} and \citet{amaral1997}, though not
the precise shape. The regular graph does not reproduce it. Its growth rates are about
an order of magnitude narrower and their flanks fall off steeply, with none of the fat
tail, because the size-dependent correlations are much weaker on a degree-homogeneous
network. So the fat-tailed form emerges from the critical dynamics only once the degree
distribution is heterogeneous, and even there the match is in shape rather than in detail.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:results-discussion}

Combining the two measurements gives a mixed answer to the question of the
introduction. A static GLV network tuned to its critical point reproduces the
non-Gaussian, fat-tailed shape of the growth-rate distribution. It does not, however,
reproduce the empirical size--variance law. As Section~\ref{sec:relaxation-transient}
shows, the model has no stationary size--variance relation at all. The measured
$\sigma(\bar S)$ is a V produced by the relaxation of an arbitrary initial condition
onto the critical attractor, and its minimum is set by where the firms start rather than by
any intrinsic scale. There is therefore no size--variance exponent to compare with the
empirical $\beta\approx0.15$--$0.20$. The slow size--variance decay is thus left unexplained on both fronts. The granular
models are rejected by the data \citep{moran2024}, and a static, critical inter-firm
network supplies the heavy tails but no stationary size--variance law. Recovering it
seems to require a genuinely nonstationary state, where size-dependent correlations of
the kind \citet{moran2024} call for can build up, an explicit noise term (a Langevin
GLV) or the chaotic $\sigma>1$ phase rather than the critical relaxation studied here.
This direction is sketched in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}.
