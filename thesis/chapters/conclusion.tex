\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis asked whether a dynamical model of interacting firms can generate the two
firm-growth anomalies on its own, without the heavy-tailed internal composition that the
granular models assume and that \citet{moran2024} show the data reject. The answer it
reaches is that it can. Working with a Generalized Lotka--Volterra network and nothing
else, no externally imposed heterogeneity, no injected noise, and no built-in
correlations, we recover both the fat-tailed growth-rate distribution and a
size--variance relation that falls with the empirical sign, as stationary properties of
a persistently fluctuating economy that grows.

The result came in two steps. A static network held at its cooperative critical point
reproduces the heavy tails on heterogeneous graphs, but its size--variance relation is
only a relaxation transient: once the shape settles onto the critical attractor the
growth rates vanish, and the apparent scaling is the trace of an initial condition
rather than a law. That failure identified what was missing, a state that keeps
fluctuating instead of relaxing, and the relative model supplies it. Making the
interactions act on the average firm rather than on absolute abundances renders the
dynamics scale-invariant, so the total abundance grows exponentially without the
finite-time blow-up of the standard model, while in the strongly disordered regime the
composition never settles. In that chaotic state both facts hold together, and they are
robust: they survive across graph realizations, system sizes, and the interval over
which growth is measured, the size--variance exponent $\beta$ falls with the empirical
sign, and its higher moments reproduce the anomalous multiscaling that the granular models
cannot. Removing the disorder removes them entirely, which
places their origin in the interactions themselves.

What this contributes is a model, not a mechanism read off from the data. The granular
and sub-unit pictures posit the source of the fluctuations, a fixed heavy-tailed
composition of parts; the mean-field multiplicative models that also give a growing
economy with heavy tails put the noise in by hand and carry no network. Here the same
phenomenology, the heavy tails, the slow size--variance decay, the growing aggregate,
and the persistent turnover of firms, follows from a single deterministic ingredient,
the disordered couplings between companies. The size-dependent correlations that
\citet{moran2024} call for are not assumed but produced, and the model makes them
something one can measure rather than a hypothesis one must accept.

Three qualifications bound the claim. The dynamics themselves are not new: the
fluctuating regime is the chaotic phase of the disordered Lotka--Volterra model,
equivalently the disordered replicator equation, whose finite-size and mean-field
properties are already studied in the literature. What is specific here is the
construction that turns it into a growing economy, the relative interactions, and the
use of that state as a model of firm growth. The quantitative match is partial: the
model reproduces the order-dependent multiscaling of the higher moments, the signature of
the size-growing correlations, but the overall magnitude of the exponent is matched only
up to the normalization of the couplings, about three times too steep under the own-degree
scaling and reaching the empirical value only at a finite economy size under
the mean-degree one.
And the growth is
near-critical: the operating point that best reproduces the facts sits close to the
boundary between a growing and a shrinking economy, so the economy grows in most
realizations but not in all of them. None of these undoes the qualitative result, but
each marks the distance between it and a quantitative theory.

The natural next steps follow from these limits. Pinning the exponent $\beta$ would need
many more realizations at the largest system sizes, where the present estimate is
noisiest. More importantly, the match shown here is in the marginal facts, the shape of
the growth distribution and the size--variance law; showing that the model produces the
specific size-dependent correlation structure that \citet{moran2024} identify, and not
merely its consequences, is the test that would tie it directly to their data, and it is
one this model is built to allow. Beyond that, replacing the schematic disordered graph
with interactions closer to the real economy, such as input--output linkages, supply
networks, and shared markets, would be the step from a model that reproduces the
stylized facts to one that speaks to the macroeconomic dynamics behind them.
